In the first chapter of *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*, Cao Cao is not a villain.

He is twenty years old, newly recommended as a filial and incorrupt candidate, and appointed chief of the northern district of Luoyang. He has five-colored rods set up at his gate, and when anyone breaks the rules — "no matter how powerful their family" — he has them beaten. He beats to death the uncle of the eunuch Jian Shuo.

This is a model official: young, upright, unafraid of powerful enemies.

In the second chapter, the famous appraiser Xu Shao gives him a verdict: "A capable minister in times of peace, a treacherous hero in times of chaos." By putting "treacherous hero" first, Luo Guanzhong has already decided how we will read him. We are told in advance to watch him turn dark.

So how does he turn?

## Starting point: a man willing to die for a cause

Chapter 4: Cao Cao borrows Wang Yun's seven-jeweled dagger and goes alone to the chancellor's residence to assassinate Dong Zhuo. The attempt fails. He pretends he came to present the blade, then escapes on the pretext of testing a horse.

This is the high-water mark of his life — a man willing to trade his life for a tyrant's. Had the story stopped here, he would be the hero.

## First crossing: Lü Boshe

Fleeing, Cao Cao is captured at Zhongmu, where Chen Gong abandons his post to follow him. They take shelter with his father's old friend Lü Boshe. Lü goes out to buy wine. Inside, Cao Cao hears knives being sharpened in the rear hall, suspects a plot against him, draws his sword, and kills eight members of the household. Only afterward does he find a pig trussed up for slaughter — the knives were for dinner.

On the road out they meet Lü Boshe returning with the wine. Cao Cao kills him too. Chen Gong, horrified: *"To kill him knowing the truth — that is the worst kind of injustice."* Cao Cao answers:

> "Better that I betray the whole world than that the whole world betray me."

The significance of the blade is not the body count. It is that Cao Cao has written himself a law: **when the possibility of betrayal exists, striking first is permitted.**

That law remained in force for the rest of his life, and its jurisdiction kept expanding.

(A note on the histories: the *Records of the Three Kingdoms* does not record this episode. Pei Songzhi's commentary quotes the *Book of Wei*, which says Lü Boshe's sons were plundering and Cao Cao acted in self-defense. The famous line comes from Sun Sheng's *Miscellaneous Records*, where the wording is "Better that I betray others than that others betray me" — and where the sentence is preceded by "then he said, grief-stricken." The novel deletes "grief-stricken" and turns a remorseful murmur into a cold manifesto. That deletion is Luo Guanzhong's first real move.)

## Second crossing: Xu Province

In 194, Cao Cao's father Cao Song is killed within Xu Province. Cao Cao raises an army for revenge, attacks Tao Qian, and "wherever he passed, he slaughtered the people and dug up the graves."

The nature of the thing has changed. Lü Boshe was a private suspicion. Xu Province is private vengeance converted into public violence against civilians. The moral bill begins to compound.

## Third crossing: embracing the emperor

In 196, Cao Cao brings Emperor Xian to Xu and rules behind the slogan "support the Son of Heaven in order to command the disobedient."

Politically it is brilliant. Morally it is decisive: **loyalty has become his instrument rather than his constraint.**

Later, in his celebrated self-defense memorial, he writes: "Had there been no me in the state, how many would have declared themselves emperor, how many kings?" The claim is true. And precisely because it is true, it is worth watching. He wants everyone to concede that what he does, though it violates propriety, conforms to fact.

A man who begins using *fact* against *propriety* is one step away from using anything at all.

## Fourth crossing: the empty food box

In 212, Xun Yu opposes Cao Cao's elevation to Duke of Wei. Cao Cao sends him a food box. Xun Yu opens it. It is empty.

Xun Yu takes poison.

This is the coldest moment in the whole process, because no blade is required. The empty box means: **you are no longer of use.**

Xun Yu was the most important partner of Cao Cao's life — the man he called "my Zhang Liang" — and the last of the old guard who genuinely believed in restoring the Han. That empty box is Cao Cao's farewell to his earlier self.

## The mechanism

Set these episodes side by side and the pattern is not a sudden change of character. It is a compounding curve of rationalization:

1. Every transgression had a good reason — survival, vengeance, pacifying the realm.
2. Every good reason made the next transgression easier to accept.
3. The lesson he drew was never *"I was wrong."* It was: **great men are not bound by small scruples.**

Hence the later set pieces:

- At Red Cliff, composing a poem with his spear across his knee, he runs the Yang Province governor Liu Fu through when Liu observes that the verse is inauspicious.
- He announces "I kill people in my dreams," then genuinely kills an attendant who came to cover him with a blanket — so that the claim will be believed.
- When supplies run out, he borrows the head of the quartermaster Wang Hou to steady the army: *"I know you are innocent. But if I do not kill you, the army will mutiny."*
- Before a campaign he executes Yang Xiu for "demoralizing the troops."

Notice that not one of these is unmotivated evil. Each has a reason, and each reason holds. That is what makes this kind of decline so frightening. It does not present itself as corruption. It presents itself as **efficiency**.

## But he did not become a pure villain

Cao Cao's deathbed testament concerns incense and slippers: he instructs his concubines to learn to make shoes and sell them, so they will not starve. It is a remarkably small, domestic worry.

After the disaster at Wan City, he mourns Dian Wei more violently than he mourns his own son — *"I lost my eldest son and my nephew and felt no deep grief; I wept only for Dian Wei."* There is real feeling in that sentence, and there is also the calculation of a man purchasing loyalty. And that is exactly Cao Cao: **his sincerity and his statecraft run on the same organ.**

His real tragedy is his late loneliness. After the rout at Red Cliff he says: *"If Guo Fengxiao were alive, this would not have happened to me."*

Guo Jia was long dead. What the sentence reveals is not regret but the recognition that **no one around him dared tell him the truth any longer** — and that he had filtered those people out himself, one by one.

## Why we remember only the "treacherous hero"

Luo Guanzhong writes Cao Cao with a labeling technique: Xu Shao's appraisal, the gossip of bystanders, the repeated recurrence of the phrase *treacherous hero*, the tuning fork of "better that I betray the world." In the novel, Cao Cao's actions arrive pre-interpreted. The reader is rarely left alone with them.

The historical Cao Cao is far more complicated than the label. He wrote "bones lie bare in the fields; not a rooster crows for a thousand *li*," and he wrote "the old warhorse in the stable still dreams of a thousand *li*." He led the Jian'an literary renaissance. He rose and wept as he watched Chen Gong walk to his execution.

So the question "how did Cao Cao become the villain" has two answers:

- **Inside the story**, he gradually converted the crossing of lines into a habit, through the compounding of rationalization.
- **Inside us**, he never turned dark at all. **We were drawn in by his charisma first and driven back by his methods second — and Luo Guanzhong merely stood at every step, reminding us which side to take.**
