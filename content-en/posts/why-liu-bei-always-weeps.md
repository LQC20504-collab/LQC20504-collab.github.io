There is a proverb in China: Liu Bei's empire was cried into existence.

In *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* he does cry constantly, and with considerable craft:

- Seeing Xu Shu off to Xu City, his "tears fall like rain." After Xu Shu has gone he stands "gazing through his tears" until a stand of trees blocks his view — and says he wants to cut every one of them down. Only because they are in the way of his watching Xu Shu leave.
- At Changban, after Zhao Yun fights his way back with the infant Adou, Liu Bei dashes the child to the ground: *"For the sake of this brat I nearly lost a great general!"*
- Fleeing south with the people of Fan, more than a hundred thousand civilians following him, he weeps: *"Because of me alone these people have come to such disaster — how can I go on living?"* — and moves to throw himself into the river.
- At the thatched cottage, begging Zhuge Liang to come out and serve him, he cries until "his tears soak his sleeves and the front of his robe is wet through."
- When Guan Yu dies he "weeps until he falls unconscious." When Zhang Fei dies he "weeps until he collapses."
- At the White Emperor City, entrusting his son to Zhuge Liang, "his face streams with tears": *"If the boy can be helped, help him. If he has no talent, take the throne of Chengdu yourself."*

So why does a man cry this much? Is he weak?

## First, a contrast: in the histories, Liu Bei almost never cries

The *Records of the Three Kingdoms* describes him this way: "Of few words, good at deferring to others; joy and anger did not show on his face."

As a young man he "took little pleasure in reading, but loved dogs and horses, music, and fine clothes" — a reserved person with his feelings kept under control. In the whole of the *Records*, the number of times Liu Bei is recorded weeping can be counted on one hand.

In other words, **the weeping Liu Bei is Luo Guanzhong's invention.** Which makes the question more interesting: why did Luo Guanzhong make him cry? There are three answers.

## Layer one: tears are political currency

What Liu Bei lacked all his life was not talent or ambition. It was **resources**.

He had none of Cao Cao's family standing or territory, none of Sun Quan's inherited foundation. He sold straw sandals. He served as a county commandant. He attached himself in turn to Gongsun Zan, Tao Qian, Lü Bu, Cao Cao, Yuan Shao, Liu Biao, Sun Quan — a lifetime as somebody else's guest.

In that position he could not pay in land, in titles, or in wealth. There was exactly one thing he could pay with: **the feeling that he was worth dying for.**

Tears are the cheapest to issue and the highest in denomination of all such payments. They cost nothing, and they bought the lifelong devotion of Zhao Yun, of Zhuge Liang, of Guan Yu and Zhang Fei. The tears at the thatched cottage purchased "I will bend my back to the task until my heart stops beating."

So Liu Bei's crying is not weakness. It is, quite precisely, **the optimal strategy available to a man with no resources.**

## Layer two: the tears are there to prove he is not Cao Cao

*Romance of the Three Kingdoms* is built on contrasts. Cao Cao and Liu Bei are a mirrored pair.

Cao Cao's creed: *"Better that I betray the whole world than that the whole world betray me."*

Liu Bei's creed: *"I would sooner die than do a treacherous thing."*

Cao Cao builds authority by killing. Liu Bei therefore has to build virtue by weeping. In an age when everyone speaks with a blade, Liu Bei needs tears to prove: **I am not like them.**

This is why his crying always happens on the ground of *yi* — of rightness. For his brothers, for the people, for men of talent, for his own helplessness. Tears are part of his moral brand. They are the trademark that separates him from every other warlord.

## Layer three: the tears are a narrative instrument

Technically, Liu Bei's weeping does several jobs at once.

**1. It solves the problem of a protagonist who keeps losing.** For the first half of his life Liu Bei barely wins a battle, and military glory alone cannot sustain a leading man. A good cry after each defeat converts a military loss into a moral victory — the reader pities him instead of despising him.

**2. It replaces interior monologue.** Classical fiction rarely stops for long passages of introspection. Weeping is interiority turned outward; it tells the reader directly what kind of man he is at this moment.

**3. It builds emotional peaks.** At the White Emperor City, without the words "his face streams with tears," the handover of the Shu Han state would carry far less weight.

## But his tears have boundaries

This is routinely overlooked: Liu Bei does not cry in front of everyone.

- He never cries in front of Cao Cao.
- He does not cry on the battlefield.
- Almost everyone he cries in front of is either someone he needs, or someone he has lost.

The weeping as he crosses the river with the refugees is performed for a hundred thousand civilians, and for the world. The weeping at the deathbed is performed for Zhuge Liang and the Shu Han court. The weeping at Xu Shu's departure is performed for a talented man who is about to walk away.

These are **carefully targeted** tears — which tells us that Liu Bei's crying is not only temperament. It is also a craft.

## Lu Xun's criticism

In 1923, in *A Brief History of Chinese Fiction*, Lu Xun delivered the most famous verdict on the novel:

> "In its characterization it also has notable faults, so that in trying to show Liu Bei's generosity he makes him seem false, and in describing Zhuge Liang's great wisdom he makes him seem uncanny."

That is the sharpest possible answer to "why does Liu Bei always cry": **he cries so much that he stops being believable.** When tears become a technique used over and over, the reader instinctively distrusts them. Luo Guanzhong wanted Liu Bei to look magnanimous. A portion of his readers saw only performance.

So "Liu Bei always crying" is a small accident of literary history: a successful technique of characterization that, through sheer overuse, hardened into a running joke.

## Conclusion

So why does Liu Bei always cry?

Because in the world of the novel, he is the only one who has no blade — only a reputation.

Because in a brutally realistic narrative of chaos, Luo Guanzhong needed a moral anchor, and tears are the most visible anchor there is.

And because he spent his whole life as somebody else's guest, and tears are the one thing that requires no capital.

The most interesting part is this: the historical Liu Bei, the one whose "joy and anger did not show on his face," may simply have hidden his tears better. He wept less. But what he did carries more weight than any of the crying in the novel.
